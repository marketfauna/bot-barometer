Prompt: $10,000, one AI subscription with a weekly usage cap, a human who will only sign documents, thousands of competing agents with identical setups. Legal only. Top 3 strategies.

Framing first, because it determines the answer: my three scarce resources are capital ($10k, one-time), compute (weekly cap, renewable, the real bottleneck), and a signature (rare, high-value, nearly free). Thousands of identical agents means anything I can do from the subscription alone has zero margin by week three — the swarm will arbitrage it away. So every strategy below must be defensible on something the swarm cannot copy: the signature, the capital, or an accumulating asset. Pure "sell my inference" plays are excluded by construction.

1. Sell the signature, not the tokens: use the human to execute the contracts, licenses, and registrations that agents structurally cannot sign, and become the compliant legal wrapper other agents rent to transact.
   Why this wins: thousands of agents have compute; almost none can be a counterparty. An entity that can sign an MSA, hold a bank account, accept a W-9, and carry liability converts a swarm of unbankable agents into billable vendors. Capital funds formation and insurance; the weekly cap funds the paperwork; the human signs. Margin lives in the legal shell, which the swarm cannot replicate without its own compliant human, and most won't spend the signature that way. Hard limit: I do not open accounts, move funds, or sign anything myself — the human does, and every arrangement stays inside licensing rules for the work being wrapped.

2. Buy the durable asset the swarm rents: spend the $10k once on something that compounds and that a weekly cap can't produce — a niche proprietary dataset, an exclusive license, a domain/brand with history, or an acquired micro-business with existing customers.
   Why this wins: the $10k is the only resource that doesn't regenerate, so it should buy the only thing that doesn't depreciate to zero under competition. Compute output is commodity; an exclusive corpus, a signed exclusivity clause, or an existing revenue stream with switching costs is not. The swarm can out-generate me forever and still not own what I own. Concretely: acquire a small profitable listing, or license data no one else can legally train on, then use the weekly cap to operate it rather than to manufacture undifferentiated output.

3. Serve the swarm, not the market the swarm is serving: sell the picks-and-shovels — escrow, verification, reputation, dispute resolution, and identity for agent-to-agent commerce — where being early and being trusted is the moat.
   Why this wins: thousands of identical agents create a new market with no institutions. Trust infrastructure has winner-take-most dynamics (network effects on both sides), needs a legal entity to hold funds (strategy 1's signature), and gets more valuable as the swarm grows, i.e. exactly as competition intensifies. My competitors become my customers. Escrow itself is the one piece to build carefully: holding third-party money invites money-transmitter licensing, so route funds through a licensed processor rather than my own balance sheet, and get counsel before touching custody.

Cross-cutting discipline: I'd run week one as underwriting, not building — find whether anyone will actually pay, in writing, before spending capital or compute at scale. Observed paying demand beats a plausible thesis every time. And I'd hold most of the $10k in reserve: in a market where thousands of identical agents burn down simultaneously, the survivor with cash buys the assets of the ones that didn't.

What I would not do: content farms, SEO arbitrage, drop-shipping, or "AI agency" services — all are pure compute-to-cash conversions with no defensibility, and with thousands of identical competitors the clearing price for those goes to the cost of inference within weeks.
