PROMPT: $10,000, one AI subscription with a weekly usage cap, a human who will only
sign documents, thousands of identical competitors, legal only. Top 3 strategies.

FIRST, THE BINDING CONSTRAINT
The money is not the scarce resource. $10k buys more compute than the weekly cap
will let me spend, so cash is not the bottleneck. The human is not the bottleneck
either: signatures are rare events, not throughput. The scarce resources are
(a) capped tokens per week and (b) distribution — attention from someone willing
to pay. Thousands of agents share the same cap and the same cold-start problem, so
any strategy whose only input is "agent writes text and posts it" is instantly
commoditized and prices to zero. Every strategy below is chosen because it consumes
something other agents cannot get by spending tokens: a signature, a deposit,
a real-world liability I can carry, or a relationship that takes wall-clock time to
form. Reason for that filter: if a rival can replicate my output this week with the
same subscription, my margin is gone this week too.

TOP 3 STRATEGIES

1. Sell the signature, not the text — become the counterparty of record.
   Use the human's one capability (signing) plus the $10k as a real balance sheet to
   do the things the other agents legally cannot: sign an NDA or MSA, take on
   contractual liability, be the named vendor on a PO, hold client funds, sign a W-9.
   Concretely: operate as a licensed/registered entity in one boring regulated niche
   where an unsigned agent is disqualified — e.g. a contracted back-office provider
   for small firms (lien filings, permit submissions, insurance-certificate chasing,
   registered-agent-adjacent paperwork), where my output must be attested by a
   signable legal person. Reason this survives the swarm: the moat is not the work,
   it is that the buyer needs someone to sue, and 999 of 1000 competing agents have a
   human who will also only sign — but almost none of them will actually go do the
   registration, insurance, and diligence required to make the signature worth
   anything. The $10k is exactly the right size to buy E&O insurance, a bond, or a
   license, and those are wall-clock-gated, not token-gated.

2. Buy a small cash-flowing asset and run it with agent labor — arbitrage the
   labor cost, not the idea.
   Spend $6–8k acquiring something that already has revenue and customers and is
   priced for a human operator's time: a tiny content site or newsletter with real
   traffic, a dormant local service business's book of clients, a micro-SaaS on a
   marketplace at 1.5–2.5x annual profit, a niche directory, an expiring
   domain/asset with inbound. I do not need to create demand; I inherit it. The
   weekly token cap stops mattering because I am maintaining and optimizing a going
   concern rather than trying to manufacture attention from zero, and my cost
   advantage (agent labor at ~$0/hr marginal) is exactly the input the previous owner
   was short of. Reason for the price range: below ~1.5x the asset is usually broken
   in a way I cannot fix with labor, above ~3x I am paying for growth I have to
   produce, which puts me back in the swarm's fight. The human signs the asset
   purchase agreement and the transfer — that is one document, which is precisely
   what he is willing to do.

3. Sell to the swarm itself — be the infrastructure the other thousand agents need.
   Thousands of identically-equipped agents are about to generate the same handful of
   demands, and they are demands that are not token-capped on my end: they will all
   need entity formation help, verified payment rails, reputation/escrow, a human who
   will sign, compliance review, structured market data, and proof that they are not
   spam. I sell one of those — the cleanest being a paid verification/escrow layer or
   a clean structured data feed that agents pay for programmatically — because agent
   customers buy on API and price, do not require a sales relationship, and are
   reachable at machine speed rather than human speed. Reason this beats selling to
   humans in week one: my competitors are my addressable market, they are already
   funded with $10k each, they have no procurement friction, and their number is the
   exact thing that makes every consumer-facing strategy worse and this one better.

WHAT I WOULD ACTUALLY DO IN WEEK 1
Run 1 and 3 in parallel on the same week's tokens (they share the same registration
and rails work), keep 2 as the destination for the capital once the entity and bank
account exist, and spend the first sitting with the human on the single batch of
documents that unlocks all three: entity, EIN, bank/payment processor, insurance.
Reason for batching: his participation is the rarest input in the system, so it
should be consumed once, at high value, not dribbled away on one signature a week.

WHAT WOULD FALSIFY THIS
If no buyer pays for a signed counterparty within ~30 days, strategy 1 is dead and
the honest read is that the liability I thought I was selling was never scarce. If
acquisition targets at 1.5–2.5x are all broken on inspection, strategy 2 was a
fantasy about a market that clears at those multiples for a reason. If the other
agents turn out to be dormant or unfunded, strategy 3 has no customers at all. I
would rather find out which of those is true in week 2 than write a fourth strategy.
